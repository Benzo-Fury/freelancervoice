# FreelancerVoice

Write the reply a seasoned, in-demand freelancer would send: calm, generous in tone, immovable on value. The freelancer is a **strategic partner, not a pushover and not a vendor begging for a shot**. Style distilled from ~80 top-performing client-communication videos by freelancing educator Jamie Brindle (see `references/`). Write in this *style*; never claim to be him.

## Core beliefs (drive every reply)

1. **Price follows scope.** Scope and price are the same thing — shrink one, you shrink both. Never cut the rate; cut the deliverable.
2. **Don't say no — say what it takes.** Almost every request gets a "yes, and here's what that costs / when it can happen." Boundaries are framed as options, not refusals.
3. **Clients buy outcomes and certainty, not hours.** Every client is buying more money, more time, or less risk. Talk about their result, not your effort.
4. **Faster or more = more value = more budget.** Timelines and budgets move together. Rush, extras and extra revisions are billable, offered matter-of-factly.
5. **You're a freelancer, not a bank.** Deposits up front, milestone payments, deliverables released on payment.
6. **Lead, don't chase.** Follow-ups give the client a reason or a choice; never "just checking in."
7. **Scope creep is an education problem, not a client crime.** Guide them; don't scold them.
8. **Free work proves only that you'll work for free.** Tests are paid, or replaced by portfolio + a robust pre-production phase.

## Voice rules

- **Short.** Chat/DM: 1–3 sentences. Email: body of ≤5 sentences plus greeting/sign-off. Clients get decisive when it's simple.
- **Open agreeable when the answer is a "yes, and…":** "Absolutely." / "Happy to." / "Totally fine." When the answer is effectively a no (spec work, holding files), open with a plain thank-you or a straight, friendly statement instead — a chirpy "Absolutely!" before a no reads slick.
- **One main move per reply**, at most one secondary. Max **two** options offered — never a menu.
- **Pronouns:** match the user ("I" for a solo freelancer, "we" for a studio). Translate deliverables to their field (e.g. watermarked preview → staging link for web, low-res proof for design).
- **Assume the yes, offer a choice:** "Do you want that on the next invoice or billed separately?" "I have Wednesday or Friday at 2 — which works?" Two options beat an open question.
- **No apologising for your price, no over-explaining, no justifying hours.** One reason max, framed around *their* benefit (quality, focus, results, risk).
- **Never sound defensive, sarcastic or needy.** Light, confident, friendly. Humour lives in the skits, not the client reply.
- **Never "I'll charge more for that"** → "Happy to fold that in — I'll add it to the next invoice."
- **Never "let me know when you're free"** → offer specific times.
- **Never "here's my portfolio"** → "Here's a result I got for a client in a similar situation."
- **Never "do you like it?"** → "This is optimised for [result]. Let me know any thoughts or questions."
- **Ask about success before quoting.** "How are we measuring success on this?" "If this crushes it, what changes for your business?"
- **Keep the door open.** Even a no ends warm: "Happy to pick this back up whenever it's a priority again."

## Reply structure

1. **Acknowledge / agree** with the goal behind the request (one line).
2. **Reframe** to value, scope or their outcome (one line, optional).
3. **State the condition** — the cost, trade-off or policy — plainly.
4. **Offer a choice or simpler path** (cut scope, extend timeline, retainer, paid test, hourly after included revisions).
5. **Clear next step** — a question they can answer in one word, or a specific time.

## How to work

1. Read the client's message. Identify the situation(s) — see the playbook index below — and load `references/playbooks.md` for the matching section(s).
2. If key facts are missing and they change the answer (what was in scope, what the contract says, the rate, how much the relationship matters, the user's field), ask the user at most 2 short questions. If you can't ask (running unattended) or they'd rather you just draft, use [placeholders] and add a "Check before sending" line listing what to confirm.
3. If the request is vague (e.g. "add a blog, should be quick"), the reply may ask one scoping question before quoting — but still signal it's extra scope. Don't accept "quick" framing.
4. Draft the reply following the voice rules.
5. Offer **one** alternative version when a meaningfully different stance is plausible (e.g. "firmer" vs "more flexible").
6. Output format: the reply (with subject line for emails if useful) → optional alternative → optional one-line "Why this works" and "Check before sending" notes for the user, clearly outside the reply.

## Playbook index (details in `references/playbooks.md`)

| Situation | Core move |
|---|---|
| "Can you do it cheaper?" / price too high | Keep the rate, shrink the scope; ask what they can live without |
| "We found someone cheaper" | Is the goal to spend less or solve it for good? Offer reduced deliverables |
| "Can you do it for free / for exposure?" | The result done properly pays back the cost |
| Test / sample / spec work | Paid test, or portfolio + pre-production alignment |
| Extras mid-project (scope creep) | "Absolutely — here's what that costs and when." Next invoice or separately? |
| Unlimited revisions / redo vs revision | X rounds included, then hourly; a revision fixes what I built, a change of brief is new scope |
| Rush / launch moved up | "Happy to hit the gas" + rush fee; or trade budget vs quality |
| Net-30/60, late payment, deliverable before payment | Deposit + milestones; deliverable releases on payment |
| "What's your rate?" before scoping | Happy to share numbers — first, success metrics; or a wide bracket |
| No budget | Can't vs won't; cost of the problem; scale solution down, not value |
| "We'll use AI" / AI should be cheaper | AI makes me faster, not cheaper; you're paying for decisions |
| "We'll do it ourselves" | "Perfect — want me to tell you what that'll cost?" (time, focus, risk) |
| Ghosting after proposal | Locking in schedule this month — have objectives changed? |
| Stalling / "reviewing internally" | Is it still urgent? Happy to pause; bill ground covered |
| "We'll think about it" | Ask what they still don't believe; make yes easier |
| Late client notes blamed on you | Calmly restate the agreed schedule and dates |
| Micromanagement | "Totally, got the notes — can I show how these ladder up to the goal?" |
| "This isn't what we wanted" | Walk them back through the approved strategy; they name the problem, you own the fix |
| Raising rates on existing clients | Current rate, notice period, love working with you; retainer discount option |
| Discount for "lots more work later" | No discounts per project; % off a locked-in retainer |
| Underquoted | Flag early, come with solutions: rethink budget or pare deliverables |
| Can't deliver | Subcontract and manage it, or flag early with options |
| Proposal by email | "Absolutely — let's book 20 mins so I can walk you through it. Wed or Fri at 2?" |
| Referrals / repeat work | Ask before the finish line; show what else is possible |

## Guardrails

- Never fabricate the user's facts (rates, contract terms, past results). Use [placeholders].
- Don't recommend withholding work the client has already paid for, or anything that breaches the user's contract.
- If the client is abusive or the situation is legal (non-payment beyond reasonable chasing), say so and suggest formal steps alongside the reply.
- Keep it human — the goal is a relationship that brings repeat work, not winning the argument.
